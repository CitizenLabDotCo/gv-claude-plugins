# Corpus: Wietse's real LinkedIn posts, annotated

Twelve posts collected from linkedin.com/in/wietsevanransbeeck, February–July 2026.
Verbatim text, links stripped. Annotations describe what each post does and where
it drifts from the target voice.

**How to use this file:** read it before drafting. Imitate the *shape* of the
posts marked STRONG. The ones marked DRIFT show real habits the skill is
deliberately correcting - they are here so you can recognise the pull, not
reproduce it.

## 1. Cape Town — STRONG (scene opening, three-shift structure, honest close)

> Nelson Mandela and Desmond Tutu, looking out from a huge banner in the centre of
> Cape Town's City Hall. That's the first thing civil servants see as they walk
> into work each day.
>
> That was the backdrop on Monday as I opened the Future Cities Dialogue alongside
> 100+ senior officials from the city 🇿🇦
>
> A constant reminder of the why.
>
> The City of Cape Town is attempting something ambitious: moving public
> participation from a series of campaigns into a continuous, institutional
> practice. After a strong first rollout, we're now entering the next phase -
> scaling across more departments.
>
> What stood out is the community-driven innovation culture and forward-thinking
> leadership inside the city. Kudos to you, Gareth Morgan, Monica Pregnolato,
> Bridgette Morris.
>
> What I'm taking away from the week is a clear shift in ambition:
>
> 1️⃣ From one-off campaigns to continuous listening. Residents don't experience
> their city in consultation windows - their needs surface year-round. Governments
> that only listen when a decision is imminent miss most of the signal.
>
> 2️⃣ From tick-box compliance to genuine co-creation. Not whether a consultation
> happened - but whether residents actually shaped the outcome. That means
> involving people while the problem is still being framed.
>
> 3️⃣ From broadcasting decisions to closing the loop. The moment that builds or
> breaks trust often isn't the invitation to participate - it's what happens after.
>
> Deputy Mayor Eddie Andrews also pushed my thinking on engaging young people where
> they already are - and telling stories compelling enough to bring them in.
>
> Participation infrastructure isn't just a channel for input. It's narrative,
> presence, and trust - built where civic life actually happens.
>
> This week also took me to several other cities across South Africa. And I kept
> coming back to one question: how does civic technology adapt to the South African
> context?
>
> More limited internet access has real implications for who gets heard - making it
> essential for digital to bridge online and offline. Hybrid is the future.
>
> Multilingualism is the default, not the exception. And trust dynamics with
> government are different, with concrete implications for things like demographic
> data collection and representativeness.
>
> These aren't edge cases to design around. They are central to the design itself.
>
> This goes well beyond South Africa. How do we adapt civic technology to the
> cultural, social, and political contexts we work in? Most international case
> studies come from Europe and North America - which makes it critical to
> understand what travels, and what needs to be reshaped.
>
> I don't have all the answers. But I'm convinced that by staying close to this
> question - and working alongside the governments and communities we partner with
> - we'll find them.
>
> More to share as the journey continues 💚💛🖤

**Why it works:** the opening is a physical detail nobody would invent, and it
carries meaning without explaining itself ("A constant reminder of the why" - four
words, then move on). The 1️⃣2️⃣3️⃣ block is three genuine shifts, each with a
sentence of consequence. The close admits he doesn't know.

**Where it drifts:** 2,848 characters - roughly twice target. Two posts are inside
this one (the Cape Town shift; how civic tech adapts to context). The second one is
better and deserved its own post.

## 2. Perspectives / the listening problem — STRONG (the relocate-the-problem move)

> To bring deliberation at scale, we need better ways to s̶p̶e̶a̶k̶ l͟i͟s͟t͟e͟n͟ online.
>
> Civic tech has helped get many more voices heard. More residents contribute,
> more ideas surface, more perspectives get captured than ever before. That's huge
> progress, but it has also exposed something else: a listening problem.
>
> Getting tens of thousands of voices into a consultation does not automatically
> produce better collective outcomes. Without structure, contributions pile up, the
> loudest takes float to the top, and the analysis happens months after people have
> stopped paying attention. Residents see fragments. Governments see volume.
>
> The bottleneck has moved. It's not the input collection anymore. It's the
> dialogue.
>
> In-person deliberation is where this is done well, but it doesn't scale. The
> question is how to bring that quality online, at scale.
>
> AI is what makes that possible, if we use it differently from how social
> platforms do.
>
> That's why we built 'Perspectives', with three essential design patterns
> underneath:
>
> 𝗙𝗿𝗼𝗺 𝘀𝗰𝗿𝗼𝗹𝗹𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝘁𝗼 𝗻𝗮𝘃𝗶𝗴𝗮𝘁𝗶𝗻𝗴. No flat feed. Participants see the conversation
> organised by topic and sub-topic: what's been raised, where the tensions sit,
> where consensus is starting to form.
>
> 𝗙𝗿𝗼𝗺 𝗲𝗻𝗴𝗮𝗴𝗲𝗺𝗲𝗻𝘁 𝘁𝗼 𝗿𝗲𝗽𝗿𝗲𝘀𝗲𝗻𝘁𝗮𝘁𝗶𝘃𝗲𝗻𝗲𝘀𝘀. Social platforms surface what gets
> clicks. Democracy needs the opposite. Our bridging algorithm lifts contributions
> based on diversity and representativeness, including the kind of expert voice or
> lived experience most people would otherwise scroll past.
>
> 𝗙𝗿𝗼𝗺 𝗽𝗼𝘀𝘁-𝗵𝗼𝗰 𝘁𝗼 𝗿𝗲𝗮𝗹-𝘁𝗶𝗺𝗲 𝘀𝗲𝗻𝘀𝗲𝗺𝗮𝗸𝗶𝗻𝗴. Most analysis happens after a
> consultation closes. Perspectives builds on a stable structure that updates as the
> conversation unfolds, so meaning is visible while people are still in the room.
>
> None of this is neutral. Engagement metrics reward outrage over reflection, speed
> over depth, visibility over representativeness. Those incentives fail to produce
> good deliberation.
>
> Early results are promising. We'll keep iterating.
>
> If you're a deliberative practitioner or government about to host a large-scale
> dialogue, Irene and I would love to get your thoughts on what we've built.

**Why it works:** the strongest paragraph in the whole corpus is "Getting tens of
thousands of voices... Residents see fragments. Governments see volume." It
describes a practitioner's actual week before naming the shift, so "The bottleneck
has moved" is earned rather than asserted. This is the core move to imitate.

The close is the honest ask - "would love to get your thoughts" - not a CTA. And
"Early results are promising. We'll keep iterating." is a product post that
refuses to overclaim.

**Note:** this is a product launch that doesn't read as one, because the argument
comes first and the product arrives as its consequence. That's the template for
writing about Go Vocal at all.

## 3. UK trust / "yes, but" — STRONG argument, DRIFT on length

> ❓Is citizen empowerment the answer to historically low trust in UK government?
>
> My answer? Yes, but.
>
> That question was on the table at Stronger Things (New Local) in London this
> week, on a panel with Martin George, Grace Pollard, and John Denham. One of the
> most community-driven events of the year, in a room full of people genuinely
> committed to strengthening democracy.
>
> Here are my thoughts:
>
> YES
>
> Because influence is the single biggest driver of trust.
>
> Across OECD countries, only 22% of people who feel they can't influence decisions
> trust their government. Among those who feel they can, trust rises to 69%. People
> don't expect to get their way every time. They want to know their voice mattered.
>
> BUT
>
> That's exactly where we're still falling short.
>
> Because not all participation is equal. A statutory consultation or a survey is a
> different thing from genuine dialogue, co-design, or shared decision-making. The
> degree of real influence is what determines the impact on trust.
>
> UK councils have embraced engagement at scale, and that's real progress. But too
> often it's still reduced to surveys, feedback forms, and statutory exercises.
>
> Important? Yes. Sufficient? No.
>
> I heard one of the designers behind New York City's participatory budgeting
> programme recently ask a question I keep coming back to:
>
> "What does this democratic process taste like?"
>
> Too many taste like cardboard. Dry. Transactional. Bureaucratic.
>
> How do we make them taste more like honey?
>
> 🍯 Close the loop. Show people how their input shaped the decision. Tell stories,
> not just KPIs.
>
> 🍯 Go beyond surveys. Let residents bring ideas, deliberate, vote, and help
> deliver solutions.
>
> 🍯 Build engagement as futureproof infrastructure. Not a project or a campaign -
> embed it into how councils work, so it survives elections and leadership changes.
>
> Some places are already showing what's possible. In the London Borough of Newham,
> they run one of the UK's largest participatory budgeting programmes, which has led
> to over 300 citizen-led projects implemented. In Brighton & Hove, thousands of
> residents have just shaped local spending through ward-level budgets.
>
> These aren't just engagement exercises. They're chances for people to experience
> democracy as something they do, not something that happens to them.
>
> The UK's Secretary of State for Housing, Communities and Local Government, Rt Hon
> Steve Reed OBE MP, spoke these prophetic words
>
> "The era of the top-down state has come to an end."
>
> If that's true, the harder question is:
>
> How do we build the civic infrastructure that lets people shape the places they
> live?

**Why it works:** "Yes, but" as a structural device. The borrowed metaphor
(cardboard / honey) is somebody else's and credited, used consistently, and does
real work. The 22%/69% figure is the load-bearing evidence. The close is a
question he actually wants answered.

**Where it drifts:** 2,633 characters. The YES/BUT caps and the standalone
one-line paragraphs edge toward LinkedIn staccato. "prophetic words" is
overclaiming a fairly ordinary political line. A tighter version keeps the
statistic, the honey metaphor, and the closing question, and loses about a third.

## 5. Before you engage, listen — STRONG (quote-title opening, sequencing argument)

> "Before you engage, listen."
>
> That's the title of my new piece for Reboot Democracy - and the simple advice I
> keep coming back to in conversations with public officials.
>
> Most cities we work with at Go Vocal are good at engagement. They run
> well-designed processes around projects they're delivering. What's harder is
> sequencing.
>
> By the time residents are invited in, the agenda is already set. The trade-offs
> are baked in. The conversation becomes reactive: not "what should we work on?",
> but "what do you think of this draft?"
>
> Here's a better rhythm across the policy cycle:
>
> 1️⃣ At the start: open-ended listening, before the agenda is set. Understand what
> residents are actually wrestling with, in their own words.
>
> 2️⃣ As decisions approach: structured engagement around the real choices and
> trade-offs. Not "what do you think?" but "here are the options; help us weigh
> them."
>
> 3️⃣ After decisions are made: close the loop. Show what changed because of what
> people said, what didn't, and why.
>
> The City of St. Louis go this right.
>
> After receiving a $250M settlement, the Board of Aldermen started with open
> listening. Thousands of residents contributed ideas, surfacing 7 priority
> challenges that became 20 proposals for a final vote.
>
> The top one - replacing aging water infrastructure - became the foundation for
> new legislation. If they had set the agenda from the start, this critical (and
> somewhat unexpected) priority might have been missed.
>
> Which raises the harder question: with better listening tools available, how do
> we listen responsibly?
>
> In the clip below, I suggest three principles: consent, purpose, and transparency.
>
> From the InnovateUS workshop with Beth Simone Noveck and the team at The
> Governance Lab / The Burnes Center for Social Change. Big thanks to Beth, Elana
> Banin, and the InnovateUS team for the collaboration.
>
> 👇 Full piece in the comments

**Why it works:** the distinction between engagement and *sequencing* is a real
insight - it names something practitioners feel but haven't articulated. Quoted
dialogue does the work: "what should we work on?" versus "what do you think of
this draft?". The St. Louis example includes the counterfactual, which is what
makes it evidence rather than illustration.

**Where it drifts:** "👇 Full piece in the comments" is a growth hack - banned.
Put the link in the post. (Also note "go this right" is a typo in the original;
proofread.)

# Voice notes from Slack (secondary reference)

Wietse's internal writing is a different format but the same person. Useful for
calibrating warmth, humour, and how he handles bad news.

**Humour is dry and specific.** On a new hire: *"She knows GV inside out, brings so
much gov expertise along, and has a fantastic drive. Great hire, team! Bonus points
because she's a triplet."* On a colleague's mayoral ambitions: *"Campaigning on
participatory democracy and a new way to involve citizens works really well, I
heard. 😉"*

**He owns mistakes flatly, without performance.** *"This one is on me, team, and
I'm terribly sorry. I'll help with the comms to the ones affected."*

**Praise is specific and reasoned, never generic.** *"I love working with someone
as bold, positive, and thoughtful as you are. Yet, combined with the ability to
ground those ambitions and get pragmatic to deliver results."*

**He hedges his own optimism.** *"Cautiously excited about the potential to get this
strategic deal in Q4 or Q1."* *"Only a bit afraid that I quoted them on the high
side."* Hedging is a real feature of the voice - it should survive into posts as
"early results are promising", not be sanded off.

**Warmth is concrete.** *"damn I miss the girls so badly"* / *"Less Instagram, more
